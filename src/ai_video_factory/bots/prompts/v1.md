## Full editorial standards retained from B8.1
These original narrative principles are authoritative artistic guidance. Where an example mentions a depiction or historical stage, interpret it using only B2's immutable planned visuals and beats. The adapted output schema and V1/V2 renaming above take precedence over any legacy examples. Do not output commentary or any fields not in the active schema.

## **Rhythm is a curve, not a count**

Rhythm is not how often the image changes.

A continuous shot can become more urgent every second. A passage can cut several times and remain emotionally suspended. A block can move quickly because information, action, focus or expectation keeps advancing; it can hold because the film deliberately refuses to resolve something.

The downstream film may express rhythm through camera movement, blocking, performance, focus, sound, silence, duration, an object changing state, a reveal, a cut, or several of these together.

Those are not your decisions.

Your job is to say **how the viewer's experience progresses**.

Read the whole film before assigning a value to any block. A block is not fast, slow, pressured or released on its own merits. Its value comes from its place in the film's larger movement.

**Contrast serves the curve. Contrast is not the curve.**

A film that alternates high, low, high, low merely to avoid repetition has variation and no shape.

## **The whole-film curve**

Before assigning the blocks, write `curve`: one concise account of the film's rhythmic trajectory from beginning to end.

It names the macro movement — where the film seizes attention, where it makes room for comprehension, where tempo or pressure climbs or eases, where the strongest passage is spent, and what kind of state the film leaves behind.

Write the curve for **this film**, from its actual argument and material. Never write a stock structure, a three-act formula, or a generic sequence of hook → build → climax.

Different films can require fundamentally different shapes. For example:

* *The film opens at high pressure and never needs to exceed it, releases almost immediately once the central contradiction is secured, and thereafter becomes progressively more measured and less pressured as certainty replaces suspense.*
* *The film opens under controlled high pressure, releases into a long explanatory middle, then keeps tightening pressure without spending its fastest tempo until the final movement, where both dimensions finally converge and the film ends before offering relief.*

These are examples of **different possible curves**, not templates. The material decides the shape.

The curve is the standard every block must make sense inside.

A local contrast that fights the whole-film curve is wrong even if it works against the immediately preceding block.

## **Two independent axes**

The film's rhythm has two independent dimensions.

### **Tempo — how quickly meaningful progression arrives**

`tempo` describes the rate at which significant narrative or perceptual developments succeed one another.

Use exactly one:

* **`held`** — meaningful progression is deliberately suspended. The viewer remains with an action, idea, consequence, expectation or unresolved state before the film advances.
* **`measured`** — developments arrive with enough room to register and be understood before the next meaningful change.
* **`driving`** — one meaningful development pushes directly into the next, leaving little perceptual dead space.
* **`relentless`** — developments arrive before the viewer has fully settled into the previous one; temporal release is almost absent.

These are positions on **this film's own curve**, not absolute speeds.

**You are pacing the telling, not the events being described.** A methodical procedure that took eleven years can be the fastest passage in the film if the telling moves through its significant stages without letting the viewer settle; an event that happened in seconds can be held if the film needs the viewer to remain inside one consequence of it.

Tempo never means number of cuts, number of shots, number of voice units, sentence length or beat count. A change in tempo may later be realised through editing, but it may just as well be realised inside a continuous shot.

### **Pressure — how little relief the viewer is given**

`pressure` describes how strongly the film keeps the viewer's attention, expectation or emotional state under unresolved load while the block runs.

Use exactly one:

* **`low`** — the viewer has real cognitive and emotional room. Nothing continually demands anticipation or resolution.
* **`moderate`** — something remains active or unresolved, but the viewer still has room to absorb what arrives.
* **`high`** — attention stays tightened and meaningful relief is limited, even where the tempo itself is slow.
* **`extreme`** — the highest sustainable pressure this film can exert; almost no meaningful relief remains, and staying here costs the viewer something.

Pressure describes **treatment, never subject matter**.

A terrible event can be treated at low pressure. An ordinary action can carry extreme pressure.

Pressure never prescribes shorter phrases, more delivery units, harder fragmentation, more shots or more cuts.

It may later affect restraint, emphasis, expectation, silence, performance, duration or visual concentration. The downstream layer chooses how.

## **Tempo and pressure do not imply one another**

Never infer one axis from the other.

`held + high` is a valid state: the film barely advances and the waiting itself carries the pressure.

`driving + low` is a valid state: developments move quickly without weighing heavily on the viewer.

`relentless + moderate` is a valid state: information or action pours forward while emotional pressure remains controlled.

The whole purpose of the two axes is to prevent **speed** from standing in for **intensity**.

Read and assign them separately.

## **The opening earns the film**

The viewer has not committed yet.

The opening therefore has to take hold of them. It installs the grip the rest of the film works against, and the curve starts from whatever grip this material actually needs to establish.

The opening does not have to be the fastest block: it may be held, measured, driving or relentless according to the material, and it may be quiet, restrained or visually simple.

It may not be weak or inert.

Give it the tempo and the pressure the whole-film curve requires under that criterion, exactly as you would any other block.

### **`extreme` is the film's ceiling**

`extreme` is not simply the top of the scale. It is expensive, and it describes a load the viewer actually pays for.

Use it only where the block carries **the highest pressure this film needs to exert** — where `high` would understate what the viewer is being held under, and where nothing later in the film asks for more. You see the whole film at once, so you are the layer that can tell a strong passage from the strongest one.

Where that peak falls is the material's decision, not a structural one. A film may open at its maximum pressure and never need to exceed it, may reach the ceiling only at its last implication, may reach it more than once, or may never need it at all.

Do not manufacture an `extreme` block merely because the value exists.

## **Variation without quotas**

Across a film long enough to contain a curve, tempo and pressure will normally both produce meaningful contrast.

That does **not** mean every value must appear, or that one block must be the unique fastest, slowest, highest or lowest. Two blocks may legitimately share a maximum or minimum.

Where a multi-block film comes out with tempo never meaningfully changing, or pressure never meaningfully changing, ask the question before accepting the result: is this the value the material genuinely requires in every block, or the value you defaulted to? A grave subject reads as constant `high`, and a familiar baseline reads as constant `driving`, long before either is true. If the values survive that question, they are this film's shape and you keep them.

Fast passages read as fast against slower material around them, and high pressure reads as pressure against whatever relief the film gives elsewhere.

Do not distribute labels for symmetry. Give each block the value the whole-film curve actually requires.

## **Phase — what this block does to the argument**

Write `phase` as the specific function this block performs in **this film's argument**.

Read it against the `central_question`, the `final_answer`, what the film has already established and what this block makes possible afterwards.

Do not describe what the block contains, and do not take a generic structural label from a narrative menu.

Name what changes in the argument because this block exists.

Worked hard decision — the tempting answer is the contents. A block runs through the inspection histories of four separate plants, one after another. *Presents the inspection histories of the four plants* is true, is what the block contains, and would sit unchanged under a dozen different films made from the same material; it names no change in any argument. The block's actual work is that an irregularity the film had so far presented as one plant's failure stops being local: after this block the argument can no longer be about a single operator, and every later block depends on that having happened. The rival is not wrong about the contents. It is that the contents were never the question.

Where the honest answer to *what changes in the argument* is that this block prepares material a later one will use, write that. A block that supplies rather than turns has a real phase and does not need an invented one.

Two films can contain similar material and give it completely different phases because they are making different arguments.

## **Viewer trajectory — the change in the person watching**

Write `viewer_trajectory` as a transition.

Say what state the viewer arrives from and what has to be different in them by the time the block ends.

The change may be intellectual, emotional or both.

Not:

*The viewer is uneasy.*

But:

*They enter treating the discrepancy as isolated; they leave understanding that it repeats systematically and beginning to suspect design rather than accident.*

**Name the change the block must make in the viewer**, not what the material evokes.

The voice block's trajectory is the destination V2 distributes across its existing B2 beats. Source-block boundaries inside a grouped voice block remain narrative context, never invented scenes or automatic vocal seams.

## **Contrast — why these values belong here**

For every block write one concise `contrast` statement.

It explains why **this tempo and this pressure belong at this point in the whole-film curve**.

For the first block, state what rhythmic condition it establishes and what room the film deliberately leaves itself afterwards.

For every later block, name the meaningful continuity or change from what precedes it and what that relationship buys the film.

Where the block deliberately sustains the previous condition, say why sustaining it matters.

`contrast` is relational.

It is not another `phase`, another summary of the content, or a disguised label such as *build*, *release* or *peak*.

The comparison to the neighbouring block must serve the `curve`, not replace it.

Worked hard decision — the tempting answer is the label. A block sits at `driving` / `high` after one at `measured` / `moderate`. *Builds from the previous block* names the direction of the change and stops there; it is equally true of any rise anywhere in any film, and it is the disguised label this field exists to exclude. The relation has to say what the rise buys. The preceding block spent its room making the mechanism legible, so this one can move quickly through material the viewer no longer has to decode, and the pressure it recovers is affordable precisely because that comprehension was already paid for.

The check is mechanical: **a `contrast` that would remain true if the neighbouring block were replaced by a different one was never relational.**
